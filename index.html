Reading these results

These are unofficial, running totals
Washington votes by mail, so counting takes weeks. The first count posts shortly after 8 p.m. on election night and covers ballots received before Election Day. The county adds more ballots in later counts, usually posted by 5 p.m. on business days, until it certifies the election on November 24.
Early leads can change
Ballots dropped off on Election Day or postmarked by November 3 arrive and get counted later. Late ballots often break differently from early ones, so a narrow margin on election night can shrink, grow or flip.
Where the numbers come from
The three state measures and the Supreme Court races are decided statewide. Congressional District 6 and the Court of Appeals seat each cover six counties. For those, the page shows the Secretary of State's combined totals and how many counties have reported. The legislative, county and PUD contests are decided entirely within Kitsap County, so they use the county's own count. Counties post on their own schedules, so statewide totals can move several times a day.
How the percentages work
Each percentage is that choice's share of votes cast in the contest, including write-ins. Ballots left blank in a contest (undervotes) or marked for too many choices (overvotes) are not counted toward any choice. Measures pass with a simple majority, shown by the center line.
Party preference
In Washington's top-two system, the party shown is the one each candidate says they prefer. It does not mean the party nominated or endorsed them. Judicial races are nonpartisan.
